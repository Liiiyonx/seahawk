\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented systems deployed in regulated domains face a reality rarely
discussed in benchmark papers: the corpus is small and grows organically, queries
fall into sharply different types, and every answer must be traceable to
authoritative text. We study this regime on a registered corpus of 27
assets---20 public marine-dumping regulatory documents from the national
environmental authority plus 7 operational assets. We first construct a domain ontology (100 nodes, 200 relations) in
which every LLM-extracted node and relation must cite its supporting passage,
and we audit the evidence chain with deterministic string verification: 96\% of
node names and 85\% of relations are fully traceable to the cited source text,
and 89.9\% of citations verify verbatim. Second, we build a 306-question
type-stratified benchmark (title-lookup, entity/content, multi-hop, refusal)
with deterministically constructed gold answers. Third, we compare six
retrieval configurations with per-question paired Wilcoxon tests and find that
\emph{no single configuration wins across query types}: plain BM25 dominates
title queries (hit@1 .901); graph-expanded lexical retrieval dominates entity
and multi-hop queries (hit@5 .930/.983); and a strong dense retriever with a
cross-encoder reranker---often assumed superior---is significantly worst on
\emph{every} type on this corpus of near-duplicate official documents. A
deterministic two-system router that sends title-coverage queries to BM25 and
everything else to the graph retriever captures the per-type oracle: it
significantly beats every single configuration (all $p<0.005$) and is
statistically indistinguishable from the per-type upper bound ($p=0.10$). For
abstention, no configuration's top-1 scores separate answerable from
out-of-domain queries (zero-floor overlap, inversion, and low-tail overlap,
respectively), cautioning against score-based refusal in small-corpus
deployments. We release the benchmark, audit tooling, router, and all
configurations.

\keywords{Domain retrieval \and Hybrid retrieval \and Adaptive retrieval \and
Knowledge ontology \and Evidence audit \and Retrieval evaluation \and

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Most retrieval research is evaluated on large, heterogeneous corpora where
dense retrieval and cross-encoder reranking are known to excel~\cite{beir,dpr}.
The deployment reality in regulated domains---environmental compliance,
permits, technical standards---is almost the opposite. The corpus is small
(tens of documents, not millions) and grows one announcement at a time;
documents are near-duplicates of each other, sharing boilerplate and titles
that differ by a single edition number; queries fall into sharply different
types, from title lookup to multi-hop reasoning across related instruments;
and, because a wrong citation can mislead a real governance decision, every
answer must be traceable to authoritative text. Small-corpus retrieval is not
merely large-corpus retrieval with fewer documents: score distributions,
ranking granularity, and the behaviour of fusion strategies all change.

This paper reports a controlled, type-stratified study of retrieval in exactly
this regime, on a real deployment: a knowledge service that backs a marine
debris governance agent for aquaculture zones. The service ingests public
regulatory documents from the national environmental authority (marine dumping
area announcements, standards, permit rules, sewage outfall guidelines, and
comprehensive governance plans), maintains a domain ontology, and answers
queries with citation-backed evidence.

We address three research questions:

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ1.} On a small, near-duplicate regulatory corpus, how do
lexical, graph-expanded, dense, and hybrid retrieval configurations compare
\emph{per query type}, with paired significance tests?

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ2.} Can an LLM-extracted domain ontology be made
evidence-traceable by construction, and audited deterministically without a
gold standard?

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{RQ3.} Is score-threshold abstention (refusing
out-of-domain queries) viable, and is any threshold portable across
configurations?

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An evidence-audit framework for LLM-extracted ontologies},
Sect.~\ref{sec:ontology}: every node and relation must cite a passage of a
registered asset; deterministic verbatim verification reports citation
verifiability (89.9\%, 302/336), relation-endpoint traceability (90.0\%), full
relation traceability (85.0\%), and node-name traceability (96\%). To our
knowledge this audit protocol---verify every fact by exact string match
against its cited source, with no model in the loop---is reusable on its
own.
\item \textbf{A type-stratified 306-question benchmark} (Sect.~\ref{sec:bench})
with four query types (title, entity/content, multi-hop, refusal) and
deterministic gold construction: zero model calls, fixed templates, gold taken
from the ontology and asset tables themselves. The corpus is a live
deployment's and grew organically during the study (15$\to$27 assets); we
discuss what that growth does to per-type conclusions in Sect.~\ref{sec:results}.
\item \textbf{A controlled comparison of six configurations with paired
significance tests} (Sect.~\ref{sec:results}): the headline finding is that
\emph{no configuration wins across types}---BM25 owns title queries, the
graph-expanded lexical retriever owns entity and multi-hop queries, and a
strong dense-plus-reranker pipeline is significantly worst on all three types;
several common assumptions (dense-plus-reranker dominance, fusion as a free
lunch) do not survive.
\item \textbf{A two-system rule router that matches the per-type oracle}
(Sect.~\ref{sec:route}): a deterministic title-coverage signal routes queries
between BM25 and the graph retriever; it significantly beats every single
configuration and is statistically indistinguishable from the per-type upper
bound, so adaptive retrieval in this regime needs two systems and one cheap
signal, not a learned router.
\item \textbf{An abstention fragility result} (Sect.~\ref{sec:route}): no
configuration's top-1 scores separate answerable from refusal queries
(zero-floor overlap, inversion, low-tail overlap), arguing for
evidence-coverage-based refusal.
\item \textbf{Released artifacts}: benchmark, all six configurations and the
router, the audit and significance tooling.\footnote{Anonymized repository:
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ask-the-regulations} (placeholder to be
created before submission).}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Hybrid retrieval.} Lexical (BM25~\cite{bm25}) and dense
retrieval~\cite{dpr,realm}, refined by cross-encoder
rerankers~\cite{monobert}, are commonly fused; reciprocal rank fusion
(RRF)~\cite{rrf} is a robust parameter-light combiner. BEIR~\cite{beir} showed
that no single retriever dominates across heterogeneous corpora; we show the
same holds \emph{within} one small domain across \emph{query types}, a
granularity usually hidden by corpus-level averages. Domain benchmarks such
as LegalBench~\cite{legalbench} measure professional reasoning at scale; our
benchmark complements them with a within-deployment, type-stratified study of
the retrieval layer itself.

\paragraph{Adaptive and routed retrieval.} Adaptive-RAG~\cite{adaptiverag}
trains a complexity classifier that routes each query to no-, single-, or
multi-step retrieval; Self-Route~\cite{selfroute} lets the model itself
decide between retrieval and long-context reading. Both target large,
heterogeneous corpora and pay for a learned or prompted router. We show that
in the small-corpus regime the router can be had for free: a single
deterministic title-coverage statistic captures the per-type oracle.

\paragraph{KG-augmented retrieval.} GraphRAG~\cite{graphrag},
LightRAG~\cite{lightrag}, and the HippoRAG line~\cite{hipporag,hipporag2}
augment retrieval with graph structures extracted from text, mostly over
large corpora. We study the complementary regime: a
small, curated, versioned ontology where graph expansion is a precision
instrument for multi-hop queries---and show it \emph{hurts} title queries
relative to plain BM25.

\paragraph{LLM knowledge construction and verifiability.} LLMs are now used to
construct knowledge graphs~\cite{zhu2024llmkg,pan2024roadmap}, but extraction
hallucination is a documented failure mode~\cite{hallucination-survey}. Our
audit is a deterministic, model-free check that every extracted fact carries a
verbatim-verifiable citation---related in spirit to grounding checks for
RAG~\cite{rag-survey} but applied to ontology construction, where the unit of
verification is a triple plus its cited evidence.

\paragraph{Abstention in retrieval and RAG.} Calibration and knowing-when-not-
to-answer are studied for LLMs~\cite{lmknow,kadavath} and for retrieval-augmented
pipelines such as Self-RAG~\cite{selfrag}. We contribute a concrete negative
result: on a small regulatory corpus, top-1 retrieval scores do not yield a
portable abstention threshold, and their failure mode differs per
configuration (zero-floor overlap, inversion, or low-tail overlap).

\section{Corpus and Evidence-Audited Ontology}
\label{sec:ontology}

\subsection{Corpus}
The evaluation corpus mirrors a production knowledge service for marine
debris governance. It contains \textbf{27 assets}: 20 public regulatory
documents harvested from two official columns of the national environmental
authority (marine dumping-area announcements, national standards and their
draft-for-comment notices, permit rules, sewage outfall technical guidelines,
and a regional comprehensive governance plan), plus 7 operational demo assets
(event records, telemetry, monitoring datasets) that the service also indexes.
Each asset is registered once and versioned immutably.

\subsection{Ontology construction with mandatory citations}
The ontology (100 nodes, 200 relations over the current published version) is
built through a candidate-extraction, human-review, publish lifecycle. The
extraction layer mixes rule-based extraction (167 relations) and LLM
extraction (33 relations). The invariant that makes the ontology auditable is
\emph{mandatory evidence}: every node and every relation must carry at least
one evidence item $\langle$asset\_id, asset\_version\_id, citation$\rangle$,
where \emph{citation} is a snippet of the cited asset's text. Ontology
publishing records the version id, so downstream decisions can pin an ontology
version.

\subsection{Deterministic evidence audit}
\label{sec:audit-method}
The audit verifies, per relation: (i) each cited snippet appears \emph{verbatim}
(normalized whitespace) in the cited asset's registered text; and (ii) both
endpoint names occur in the text of a successfully verified citing document.
Per node: the node name (or an alias) occurs in its source document. All checks
are exact string operations over the same registered corpus---no model calls,
no manual annotation claims. We deliberately call this an
\emph{evidence-traceability audit}, not a gold-standard precision/recall
evaluation: a fully traceable relation can still be domain-silly, and recall
of ``all true facts'' is undefined without expert annotation.

The benchmark has 306 questions in four types (Table~\ref{tab:bench}),

\paragraph{F1: No configuration wins across query types.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} is the core result, and the specialisation is sharp.
Plain BM25 owns title queries: hit@1 .901 and hit@5 1.000, significantly
above every other system (vs.\ Lex+Graph $p{=}4.8{\times}10^{-8}$, vs.\
RRF Hybrid $p{=}9.0{\times}10^{-4}$)---title matching is exactly what BM25's
term weighting does. The graph-expanded lexical retriever owns the other two
types: entity hit@5 .930 (vs.\ BM25 $p{=}1.3{\times}10^{-2}$) and multi-hop
hit@5 .983 (vs.\ BM25 $p{=}1.6{\times}10^{-3}$)---when the query names
entities rather than titles, graph expansion supplies the vocabulary bridge.
Each specialist loses where the other wins (Lex+Graph drops to .519 hit@1 on
titles; BM25 drops to .840 and .950 hit@5 on entity and multi-hop), and the
deltas are significant on both sides.

\paragraph{F2: Dense retrieval with cross-encoder reranking is significantly
worst on every type.} The configuration most often recommended by default---
bi-encoder retrieval plus a cross-encoder---loses significantly on all three
types: title $p{=}.014$, entity $p{<}10^{-6}$, multi-hop $p{<}10^{-7}$,
each vs.\ Lex+Graph. Its title hit@1,
hit@3 and hit@5 are all .481: the gold document is either ranked first or
missing from the reranker's candidate window entirely. The mechanism is
structural: this corpus contains clusters of near-duplicate official
documents; whole-chunk embeddings dilute the few discriminative tokens (an
edition year, a place name), and the reranker can only reorder the dense
top-20 chunks. Dense retrieval's known weakness on
near-duplicates~\cite{beir} dominates when the \emph{entire corpus} is
near-duplicates of a few families.

\paragraph{F3: Fusion is a strong single system, not a free lunch.} RRF
fusion of the graph retriever with the dense channel lifts title queries far
above graph-only retrieval (hit@1 .519$\to$.815, $p{=}1.1{\times}10^{-7}$)
while preserving multi-hop (.983 hit@5), which makes it the best
\emph{single} deployable configuration. But it remains significantly below
the title specialist (BM25 .901 hit@1, $p{=}9.0{\times}10^{-4}$) and below
the graph specialist on entity queries (.860 vs.\ .930 hit@5,
$p{=}4.0{\times}10^{-3}$); title-boosting the dense channel (TB Hybrid)
adds little over plain RRF. Fusion averages toward the specialists instead
of reaching them---the gap is exactly what routing should recover.

\paragraph{F4: The deployment regime ages benchmarks.} The corpus grew
organically during system development (15$\to$27 assets, the added documents
being highly similar dumping/outfall announcements), and every absolute
number moved with it. Corpus and benchmark changed together, so we treat
growth as a property of the regime rather than a controlled variable---and
type-stratified reporting is what keeps such drift visible instead of hiding
it in an average.

\section{A Two-System Router and Abstention}
\label{sec:route}

\paragraph{The rule router captures the oracle.} The oracle that routes each
query type to its best system (title $\to$ BM25, entity and multi-hop $\to$
Lex+Graph) reaches overall hit@5 .970 / MRR .855. The rule router approximates
it with one deterministic signal---character-bigram overlap ${\ge}0.5$
between the query and any registered asset title---because title coverage is
exactly what separates the type BM25 wins from the types graph expansion
wins. It routes 73/81 title queries to BM25 and sends everything else to the
graph retriever (13 non-title queries are misrouted to BM25); overall routing
accuracy is 93.0\% (280/301 answerable queries). The full routing confusion
is compact (Table~\ref{tab:confusion}): errors concentrate on multi-hop
queries whose text happens to quote an asset title. The result
(Table~\ref{tab:main}, last row): overall hit@5 .970 / MRR .848, with
per-type numbers at or above each specialist's own (multi-hop hit@1 .808,
slightly above Lex+Graph's .800---some misroutes happen to help). Paired
tests show the router \emph{significantly beats every single configuration}
(vs.\ Lex+Graph $p{=}2.3{\times}10^{-7}$, vs.\ BM25 $p{=}3.3{\times}10^{-4}$,
vs.\ both hybrids $p{<}5{\times}10^{-4}$) and is statistically
indistinguishable from the per-type oracle ($p{=}0.10$). This mirrors the
routing philosophy of Adaptive-RAG~\cite{adaptiverag} with a regime-
appropriate simplification: where large heterogeneous corpora need a trained
complexity classifier, here one deterministic string statistic suffices. All
highlighted
comparisons in Tables~\ref{tab:sig} and~\ref{tab:confusion} survive Holm
correction within their test families (largest adjusted $p$ .013 for the
specialist contrasts; $9.9{\times}10^{-4}$ for the router family). A post-hoc
threshold sweep (0.1--0.9; artifact) shows the router is not knife-edge:
overall hit@5 stays within .95--.97 for any cutoff in $[0.2, 0.5]$. The sweep
also reveals that a lower cutoff (0.2, routing 194/301 queries to BM25) would
yield slightly higher MRR than our a-priori 0.5 ($+.023$, paired
$p{=}.022$)---we keep the a-priori value to avoid post-hoc tuning and
recommend a per-domain sweep in practice. The practical
reading for small-corpus deployments: adaptive retrieval here
needs two indexes and one string-overlap signal---not a learned router, not
fusion.

\paragraph{Abstention: no configuration separates.} With only 5 refusal
queries this is a case study, not a powered experiment---but the pattern is
consistent and stark (Table~\ref{tab:refuse}, Fig.~\ref{fig:refuse}). Under Lex+Graph, four
answerable queries return \emph{no lexical hit at all} (top-1 score exactly
0) while refusal queries score up to 46.5: the answerable low tail reaches
the floor of the scale, far below the refusals---a zero-floor overlap. Under
BM25 the boundary \emph{inverts}: one out-of-domain question (13.7)
outscores the weakest answerable questions (min 7.4)---BM25 rewards long
out-of-domain queries that match high-IDF bigrams. Under dense scores the
refusal cluster ($1.1{-}3.1{\times}10^{-3}$) sits inside the answerable
low tail (min $8{\times}10^{-4}$). No cutoff separates answerable from
refusal under \emph{any} configuration, and a threshold tuned on one
configuration transfers to none. Our production QA layer therefore abstains
on \emph{evidence coverage} (are the retrieved citations sufficient to
ground an answer?) rather than retrieval score, and states its refusal to
the user.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\emph{Single domain, single language.} The corpus is Chinese regulatory text;
the benchmark templates and the near-duplicate structure are domain-shaped.
Replication on a second regulatory domain is future work; the construction
recipe (Sect.~\ref{sec:bench}) is domain-agnostic.

\emph{Refusal pool is small} (5 queries), so Table~\ref{tab:refuse} is
evidence of fragility, not a powered estimate of abstention error rates.

\emph{Run-to-run stability.} All comparisons are paired within one frozen
evaluation run with warm caches; we anchor claims on within-run paired tests
rather than stability of absolute decimals across reruns.

\emph{Audit is not a gold standard.} Traceability verifies that extracted
knowledge quotes its source, not that the knowledge base is complete or
domain-correct (Sect.~\ref{sec:audit-method}).

\emph{Router scope.} The title-coverage signal is cheap and language-light,
but its $0.5$ cutoff reflects this corpus's title conventions; the sensitivity
sweep (Sect.~\ref{sec:route}) is the calibration recipe for a new domain, and
neural-sparse or late-interaction retrievers (SPLADE, ColBERT) were not
evaluated---the comparison targets the configurations actually deployed.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On a small, near-duplicate regulatory corpus---the regime real governance
deployments live in---no retrieval configuration wins across query types:
plain BM25 owns title lookup, graph-expanded lexical retrieval owns entity
and multi-hop queries, and a strong dense-plus-reranker pipeline is
significantly worst on every type. A deterministic two-system router---title
coverage to BM25, everything else to the graph---is statistically
indistinguishable from the per-type oracle and beats every single system.
No configuration's scores separate answerable from refusal queries. The
practical recipe our deployment adopted is: keep the ontology
evidence-audited, put BM25-honest title matching behind a one-signal router,
and abstain on evidence coverage rather than retrieval score. We release
everything needed to re-run this study on a new domain.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility.} The frozen run artifact, benchmark,
ablation, router, audit, and significance scripts are in the anonymized
repository (footnote 1). Every model call is cached; all numbers regenerate
deterministically from the frozen snapshot. During preparation we found and
fixed a benchmark-builder bug that emitted non-unique question ids (306 items
mapping to only 22 ids), silently corrupting id-keyed per-item analyses; all
numbers in this paper come from the corrected benchmark, and the pre-fix
artifacts are retained alongside for provenance.
